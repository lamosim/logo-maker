# Empirical Science of Logo Design Effectiveness — Research Memo for an AI Logo Workflow

**Compiled:** 2026-10-02 from ~45 searches/scrapes (Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, IDEAS/RePEc, PubMed, publisher pages, arXiv). Findings were checked against each paper's published abstract or text unless marked **[not re-verified]** (from memory or a citing source). Confidence: **strong** = replicated across labs/top journals with a known mechanism; **moderate** = one multi-study top-journal paper or several weaker ones; **weak** = single/low-tier/industry study or a rule derived by analogy.

Entry format: citation · method · finding · direction · **design rule** · *flips*.

---

## A. Foundational design dimensions

**1. Henderson & Cote (1998), "Guidelines for Selecting or Modifying Logos," *Journal of Marketing* 62(2):14–30, DOI 10.1177/002224299806200202.** Method: 195 logos rated on 13 design characteristics reduced to dimensions — *natural* (representative + organic), *harmony* (symmetry + balance), *elaborate* (complex + active + depth), plus parallel, proportion, repetition, round — regressed on correct recognition, false recognition, affect, and familiar meaning. Findings: natural, harmony, and elaborate carry most variance. Correct recognition and affect rise with naturalness and *moderately high* elaborateness; harmony is slightly inverted-U (very low and very high harmony are less recognizable). *False* recognition (believing you have seen a new logo) is driven by the opposite profile — low naturalness, high harmony, parallel lines, height ≈75–80% of width — i.e., generic features. Correct and false recognition are uncorrelated (r = .05). Repeated elements aid recall with heavy exposure. **Design rule:** default to the "high-recognition" profile: representational/organic form, balanced but not mirrored, moderately elaborate, one repeated element. Avoid the "low-investment" profile (abstract, highly symmetric, parallel, ~3:4) unless the goal is cheap generic familiarity. *Flips:* with no media budget and directory-style choice, false recognition can be rational. Van der Lans et al. (2009, *Marketing Science* 28(5):968–985, DOI 10.1287/mksc.1080.0462) replicated across 10 countries that elaborateness, naturalness, and harmony are universal dimensions, with country-specific weights **[weights not re-verified]**. Confidence: **strong**.

**2. Machado, de Carvalho, Torres & Costa (2015), "Brand logo design: examining consumer response to naturalness," *Journal of Product & Brand Management* 24(1):78–87.** Method: 96 logos classed abstract / cultural (man-made referents) / organic; two surveys, n = 220. Finding: natural > abstract on affect; organic > cultural; affect toward *unknown organic* logos equaled that toward *well-known abstract* logos. **Design rule:** for new brands, prefer organic/representational forms; abstract marks need exposure to earn equal liking. *Flips:* concrete logos read as "old brand" (#16). Confidence: **moderate**.

**3. Pittard, Ewing & Jevons (2007), "Aesthetic theory and logo design: examining consumer response to proportion across cultures," *International Marketing Review* 24(4):457–473, DOI 10.1108/02651330710761026.** Survey in Australia, Singapore, South Africa. Golden-ratio (1:1.618) preference was similar across cultures *but only for nature-based forms*; for geometric/artificial logos 1:1 was preferred; few ratios tested. **Design rule:** ≈1:1.6 bounding box for organic marks, ≈1:1 for geometric marks; do not treat the golden ratio as universal (the broader psychophysics literature is mixed; Green 1995, cited by the authors). Note Henderson & Cote: 75–80% height/width raises *false* recognition. Confidence: **weak-moderate**.

## B. Shape, symmetry, structure

**4. Jiang, Gorn, Galli & Chattopadhyay (2016), "Does Your Company Have the Right Logo?…," *Journal of Consumer Research* 42(5):709–726, DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucv049.** Five experiments (same shoe ad, circular vs angular vs no logo). Circular → "softness" (comfortable, caring, customer-sensitive); angular → "hardness" (durable, strong). Mechanism is visual imagery: effect vanished under visual working-memory load, not verbal load. **Design rule:** round for comfort/care/warmth (sincerity); angular for durability/strength/performance (ruggedness, competence). *Flips:* weakens in cluttered contexts that block imagery. Confidence: **strong**.

**5. Bajaj & Bond (2018), "Beyond Beauty: Design Symmetry and Brand Personality," *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 28(1):77–98, DOI 10.1002/jcpy.1009.** Four experiments: asymmetry → higher perceived *excitement*, partly via arousal; symmetry still liked more. **Design rule:** add controlled asymmetry only when "exciting" is a target trait. Confidence: **strong**.

**6. Luffarelli, Stamatogiannakis & Yang (2019), "The Visual Asymmetry Effect," *Journal of Marketing Research* 56(1):89–103, DOI 10.1177/0022243718820548.** Archival (Interbrand valuations, Y&R ratings, crowd-rated symmetry) plus experiments with symmetric/asymmetric versions of one logo. On average symmetric logos ↔ higher evaluations and valuation; for exciting brands the relation reverses and equity *rises* with asymmetry; matching the *degree* of asymmetry to the degree of excitement maximizes preference. Brands do not exploit this in practice. **Design rule:** default to balance; dial asymmetry up in proportion to brand excitement. *Flips:* sincere/competent/traditional brands lose equity with asymmetry. Confidence: **strong**.

**7. Bettels & Wiedmann (2019), *Journal of Business Research* 97:1–9, DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2018.12.039.** Asymmetry ↔ excitement; logo liking depends on fit with the consumer's self-concept and spills over into inferred product aesthetics/symbolism more than functionality. **Northey & Chan (2020), *JBR* 115:149–159, DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2020.04.049:** conservatives (intuitive processors) dislike asymmetric logos more than liberals (4 studies incl. click-through). **"Beauty of symmetry" (2025, open access, PMC11774398; authors not captured):** symmetric logos → perceived stability → higher perceived product quality, only under low cognitive load. **Design rule:** choose symmetry with the target customer's self-image and ideology in mind; symmetry is a cheap quality cue in calm contexts. Confidence: **moderate**.

**8. Fajardo, Zhang & Tsiros (2016), "…The Case of Brand Logo Frames," *JCR* 43(4):549–566, DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucw048.** Six studies: a frame reads as *protection* when purchase risk is salient (↑ intent) and as *confinement* when risk is low (↓ intent). **Design rule:** enclose the mark (shield, badge) for risky categories (finance, security, health); keep hedonic/low-risk marks open. Confidence: **moderate-strong**.

**9. Stability vs instability.** **Rahinel & Nelson (2016), *JCR* 43(3):478–496, DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucw039:** unstable-looking logos make the environment feel unsafe, raising utility of *safety* products. **Huang, Cai & Xu (2021), *Journal of Brand Management* 28(5):465–480, DOI 10.1057/s41262-021-00238-6:** stable logos preferred for "conservation" brand concepts, unstable for "openness" (fluency of match). **Zhou & Chen (2026/27), "Leaning into risk: How slanted typeface logos increase perceived risk of efficacious products," *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* 95:105115, DOI 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105115:** slanted logotypes on efficacy products (energy drink, sunscreen) raise perceived risk via design instability — amplified under cognitive load, reducing incentive-compatible choice; arousal, movement, and fluency were ruled out. **Mead, Richerson & Li (2020), *Journal of Retailing* 96(2):282–296:** right-slanted fonts help promotional retail ads **[not re-verified]**. **Design rule:** level baselines, grounded forms, upright type for trust/safety/efficacy and conservation brands; tilt and precariousness only for openness/excitement brands and promotions. Confidence: **moderate-strong**.

**10. Eyni, Thongpapanl, Ashraf & Wang (2023), "The logo 'visual thickness effect'," *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* 22(2):343–364, DOI 10.1002/cb.2133.** Five studies, >4,000 participants, 20 fictitious logos: thicker strokes → perceived brand *power* → stronger personality perceptions; weaker for high-power or high-visuospatial consumers. **Design rule:** heavy weight for power/competence/ruggedness; light weight for delicacy/sophistication. Confidence: **moderate**.

**11. Togawa & Onzo (2026), "The Three-Dimensional Effect in Logo Design," *JMR*, DOI 10.1177/00222437261460880.** Field, lab, online: depth cues → realism → trust → purchase likelihood, but only in visually simple environments and for tangible offerings; no advantage in clutter or for abstract services. **Design rule:** flat by default; subtle depth only for physical products on clean backgrounds. Confidence: **moderate**.

## C. Meaning: descriptiveness, concreteness, image vs text

**12. Luffarelli, Mukesh & Mahmood (2019), "Let the Logo Do the Talking: The Influence of Logo Descriptiveness on Brand Equity," *JMR* 56(5):862–878, DOI 10.1177/0022243719845000.** Six studies incl. 597 real logos. More descriptive logos (elements indicating the product category) → easier processing → authenticity → better evaluations, purchase intent, and financial performance. Effect is much weaker for familiar brands and *reverses* in negatively valenced categories (e.g., pest control, funerals). No moderation by logo type (wordmark/icon/mixed), goods vs services, brand age, or personality; practitioners under-use descriptiveness. **Design rule:** for new brands in positive categories, include a category cue. *Flips:* drop it for unpleasant categories; little value for famous brands. Confidence: **strong**.

**13. Morgan, Fajardo & Townsend (2021), "Show it or say it," *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* 49(3):566–583, DOI 10.1007/s11747-020-00760-0.** Several hundred global brands + 3 experiments: unfamiliar brands do better with *text-based* logos, familiar brands with *image-based* logos (fluency). **Design rule:** new brands lead with a legible wordmark (optionally a small descriptive symbol); earn the symbol-only mark later. Confidence: **moderate-strong**.

**14. Zheng & Zhu (2026), "Visualizing brand longevity: How logo concreteness shapes consumer perceptions of brand age," *JRCS* 88:104495, DOI 10.1016/j.jretconser.2025.104495.** Five studies: concrete logos → brand perceived as older; preferred for traditional positioning, abstract logos for modern positioning. **Design rule:** heritage → representational; tech/modern → abstract. Confidence: **moderate**.

**15. Hagtvedt (2011), "The Impact of Incomplete Typeface Logos on Perceptions of the Firm," *Journal of Marketing* 75(4):86–93, DOI 10.1509/jmkg.75.4.86.** Three studies: letterforms with missing parts → lower trustworthiness (via clarity) but higher innovativeness (via interestingness); overall attitude drops for prevention-focused, not promotion-focused, consumers. **Design rule:** cut-out/negative-space letters only when innovation matters more than trust; avoid for finance, health, insurance. Confidence: **strong**.

## D. Complexity, fluency, exposure, memory

**16. Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman (2004), *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 8(4):364–382, DOI 10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_3.** Figural goodness, figure–ground contrast, repetition, symmetry, and prototypicality raise liking because they ease processing. **Montoya et al. (2017), *Psychological Bulletin* 143(5):459–498:** meta-analysis of 268 exposure curves — mere exposure is an inverted-U for visual stimuli (liking rises, then declines with over-exposure). **Design rule:** maximize contrast and figure–ground clarity; stay near category prototypes unless distinctiveness is the explicit goal. Confidence: **strong**.

**17. Van Grinsven & Das (2016), "…Brand logo complexity moderates exposure effects on brand recognition and brand attitude," *Journal of Marketing Communications* 22(3):256–270, DOI 10.1080/13527266.2013.866593.** Two experiments (n = 68, 164): exposure raised recognition speed and attitude *especially for complex logos* — short-term benefits for simple logos, long-term benefits for complex ones. Janiszewski & Meyvis (2001, *JCR* 28(1):18–32, DOI 10.1086/321945) show repetition/spacing fluency gains depend on complexity **[interaction direction not re-verified]**. **Design rule:** launch-stage/low-budget → simple; sustained exposure → complexity is affordable and pays more. Confidence: **moderate-strong**.

**18. Complexity as a signal.** **Mahmood, Luffarelli & Mukesh (2019), *Journal of Business Venturing* 34(1):41–62, DOI 10.1016/j.jbusvent.2018.09.006:** complex logos feel novel and signal venture *innovativeness*, increasing crowdfunding (survey, field, experiment). **Tang, Huang & Zhang (2025/26), "Simple or complex logos?…," *International Journal of Research in Marketing* 43(2):264–277, DOI 10.1016/j.ijresmar.2025.04.007:** seven studies — complexity → perceived craftsmanship → luxuriousness (and social-media follows); attenuated when craftsmanship is already stated and for familiar brands. **Chen, Liu, Feng & Meng (2025), *JBR* 200:115580, DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2025.115580:** simple logos → *competence* (psychologically distant); complex → *warmth* (closer); preference depends on consumption goal and personality. **Bossel, Geyskens & Goukens (2019), *Food Quality and Preference* 71:129–135, DOI 10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.06.009:** simple flat logos increased food intake for basic products; complex logos did so for prestigious products. **Design rule:** simplicity for competence, fast recognition, basic/low-involvement products; ornament/craft detail for luxury, innovation signaling, warmth — given enough exposure, and never for familiar brands that already own quality. Confidence: **moderate-strong**.

**19. Memorability — state of evidence.** No peer-reviewed memorability model trained on logos was found. General image-memorability work (LaMem/AMNet CNNs; Needell & Bainbridge 2022; ViTMem, arXiv 2301.08647; autoencoder models, *Scientific Reports* 2026 / arXiv 2410.15235; Davis & Bainbridge 2023, *PNAS* 120(28) for artwork) shows memorability is an intrinsic, observer-consistent property, predictable near the human ceiling, and tied to *distinctiveness* (atypical, high-reconstruction-error images are remembered). Industry: Signs.com "Branded in Memory" (2017, n = 156, not peer-reviewed) — only ~6% drew a near-perfect Starbucks logo; people kept color and the dominant shape, not detail. A 2025 neuromarketing study (*Behavioral Sciences* 15(4):502) found the logo with a distinctive icon had the highest recall vs text-only. **Design rule:** one dominant distinctive silhouette + one owned color; test with draw-from-memory or 1-second recognition; prototypical marks score on *false* recognition, not memory. Confidence: **moderate** (by inference).

## E. Dynamism

**20. Cian, Krishna & Elder (2014), "This Logo Moves Me," *JMR* 51(2):184–197, DOI 10.1509/jmr.13.0023.** Static logos evoking perceived movement raise engagement and attitude, moderated by congruence between the implied movement and brand characteristics. **Brasel & Hagtvedt (2016), "Living brands," *JAMS* 44(5):639–653, DOI 10.1007/s11747-015-0449-2:** agent animation improves attitudes toward dynamic firms but hurts stable firms; strongest when consistent with slogan and graphic; replicated with choice. **Baxter & Ilicic (2018), *IJRM* 35(3):509–523, DOI 10.1016/j.ijresmar.2018.03.002:** depicting a drag force raises perceived brand work/energy → attitude and behavior; thrust cues attenuate. **Wang, Liu & Chen (2023), *JBR*:** dynamism pays more for hedonic than utilitarian products **[citing excerpt]**. **Design rule:** imply motion (diagonals, trailing elements, forward lean) for exciting/hedonic/dynamic brands; static, grounded forms for stability/competence/utilitarian brands. Confidence: **moderate-strong**.

## F. Color

**21. Labrecque & Milne (2012), "Exciting red and competent blue," *JAMS* 40(5):711–727, DOI 10.1007/s11747-010-0245-y.** Four studies on Aaker's scale: red ↔ excitement (β = .53); blue ↔ competence; saturation ↑ excitement (β = .20); lightness ↓ ruggedness (β = −.34). Color-induced personality raised purchase likelihood when it matched the intended personality; color and shape combined additively. The paper also links black/purple to sophistication, brown to ruggedness, white/pink to sincerity **[not re-verified]**. **Design rule:** pick hue by target dimension; saturation is the excitement dial, darkness the ruggedness/competence dial. Confidence: **strong** (red/blue, saturation, value); **moderate** (other hues).

**22. Madden, Hewett & Roth (2000), *Journal of International Marketing* 8(4):90–107.** Eight-country survey: cross-cultural similarity *and* dissimilarity in color meaning; blue, green, white broadly liked with shared calm/peaceful meanings, red consistently active/hot, other hues variable **[hue specifics from memory]**. **Design rule:** blue/green/white travel best; verify red, yellow, purple, black per market. Confidence: **moderate**.

**23. Sundar & Kellaris (2017), *Journal of Business Ethics* 146(3):685–701, DOI 10.1007/s10551-015-2918-4:** green or blue logos → eco-friendliness → more ethical judgments of the retailer. **Yue & Xia (2026), "The color of status," *JRCS* 88:104564, DOI 10.1016/j.jretconser.2025.104564:** six studies (N = 2,274, incl. field) — black-and-white logos → coolness → higher brand status and WTP; attenuated for familiar brands and warmth-positioned brands. **Hagtvedt (2020), *Psychology & Marketing* 37(7), "Dark is durable, light is user-friendly"** (title states finding). **Hagtvedt & Brasel (2017), *JCR* 44(2):396–413:** saturation increases perceived size. **Design rule:** monochrome for new premium brands; green/blue for sustainability/ethics; dark for durability, light for friendliness; saturated color for energy. *Flips:* monochrome does not help warm or famous brands. Confidence: **moderate**.

**24. Brand gender.** **Lieven et al. (2015), *European Journal of Marketing* 49(1/2):146–169, DOI 10.1108/EJM-08-2012-0456:** logo shape, name, font, and color shift masculinity/femininity, which predicts preference and equity beyond other personality dimensions. **Machado, Fonseca & Martins (2021), *Journal of Brand Management* 28(2):152–170, DOI 10.1057/s41262-020-00216-4:** cultural/geometric designs → masculine, organic → feminine; dark blue reinforces masculine, light pink feminine; both raise affect. **Design rule:** angular/geometric + dark/heavy + uppercase = masculine; organic + light/pastel + lowercase = feminine. Confidence: **moderate**.

## G. Typography and name

**25. Henderson, Giese & Cote (2004), "Impression Management Using Typeface Design," *Journal of Marketing* 68(4):60–72, DOI 10.1509/jmkg.68.4.60.42736.** 210 typefaces; four impression factors (pleasing, engaging, reassuring, prominent). Elaborate ↑ engaging but ↓ pleasing/reassuring; harmony ↑ pleasing/reassuring but ↓ engaging/prominent; natural (script-like) ↑ pleasing/engaging but ↓ prominent; flourish ↑ pleasing; heavy weight ↑ prominent (strong, masculine); moderate compression most pleasing. No font is high on all four. **Design rule:** trust brands = simple, harmonious, mildly natural; creative brands = more elaborate/active; power brands = heavy weight. Confidence: **strong**.

**26. Grohmann, Giese & Parkman (2013), "Using type font characteristics to communicate brand personality of new brands," *Journal of Brand Management* 20(5):389–403.** Three experiments: naturalness, harmony, flourish raised *all five* Aaker dimensions (β ≈ .10–.22); weight raised ruggedness (β .09–.17) and competence (.05) but lowered sophistication (−.06); elaborateness raised excitement (.12) but lowered competence (−.05). "Light, extended fonts make the brand appear more sincere, exciting and sophisticated (but less rugged and competent)." Font color effects were small. **Design rule:** weight is the rugged/competent-vs-sophisticated lever; elaborateness trades competence for excitement; harmony + naturalness + modest flourish lifts everything. Confidence: **moderate-strong**.

**27. Doyle & Bottomley (2006), "Dressed for the Occasion: Font-Product Congruity in the Perception of Logotype," *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 16(2):112–123, DOI 10.1207/s15327663jcp1602_2** (also 2004, *JBR*, "Font appropriateness and brand choice"). Font appropriateness depends on matching font and product on Osgood *potency* and *activity*, plus the font's own evaluative tone; appropriate fonts changed which company people chose. **Zhang, Teng, Xie, Wang & Foti (2025), "Serif or sans serif typefaces?…," *European Journal of Marketing* 59(4):879–922, DOI 10.1108/EJM-06-2023-0497:** five studies — serif logos → *activity*; sans-serif → *potency*; serif fits hedonic/open brands, sans-serif utilitarian/controlled ones. A low-tier study (Wang, Shanat & Kanyan 2022) found serif more complex and feminine, sans-serif more masculine, no luxury difference. **Design rule:** sans-serif for utilitarian/potent/competent/masculine; serif for hedonic/lively/sophisticated/feminine; check potency and activity fit, not just taste. Confidence: **moderate**.

**28. Case, spacing, handwriting.** **Wen & Lurie (2018), *Journal of Retailing* 94(4):393–407, DOI 10.1016/j.jretai.2018.10.002:** lowercase → feminine, uppercase → masculine; congruity with the benefit's gender raises evaluations. **Yu et al. (2022), *Journal of Retailing* 98(2):335–355:** all-uppercase names read as more premium **[abstract partly verified]**. **Gupta & Hagtvedt (2021), *JCR* 48(3):474–491:** tight letter spacing signals safety/togetherness, loose spacing reduces perceived safety, moderated by cultural tightness **[citing excerpt]**. **Schroll, Schnurr & Grewal (2018), *JCR* 45(3):648–672, DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucy014:** handwritten type → human presence → attachment → evaluations/purchase (30.4% vs 5.6% in one field study); no effect for functional products or already-attached brands. **Design rule:** uppercase + tight tracking for premium/masculine/safety; lowercase for approachable/feminine; handwritten only for emotional, artisanal, new brands. Confidence: **moderate**.

**29. Cross-modal congruence.** **Klink (2000), *Marketing Letters* 11(1):5–20:** front vowels (/i/, /e/) connote small, light, fast, sharp, feminine; back vowels (/o/, /u/) large, heavy, slow, strong; fricatives softer than stops. **Klink (2003), *Marketing Letters* 14(3):143–157, DOI 10.1023/A:1027476132607:** front-vowel names pair with smaller, more angular, lighter marks; marks consistent with the name communicate meaning better. **Lowrey & Shrum (2007), *JCR* 34(3):406–414; Shrum et al. (2012), *IJRM* 29(3):275–279:** the preference holds only when the connoted attribute is desirable for the category (front vowels for convertibles/knives, back vowels for SUVs/hammers), across English, French, Spanish, Chinese. **Velasco et al. (2014, *Food Quality and Preference* 34; 2015, "The taste of typeface," *i-Perception* 6):** rounded typefaces/shapes/names ↔ sweet, angular ↔ sour/bitter; rounder typefaces liked more and judged easier to read. **Design rule:** make name sound, mark shape, and typeface agree ("ee"-names → lighter, crisper marks; "o/u"-names → rounder, heavier, darker marks; round type for sweet/soft categories). Congruence beats any single feature. Confidence: **moderate-strong**.

## H. Redesign

**30. Walsh, Winterich & Mittal (2010), "Do logo redesigns help or hurt your brand? The role of brand commitment," *JPBM* 19(2):76–84, DOI 10.1108/10610421011033421.** Field experiment, n = 632 (New Balance, Adidas): the larger the increase in roundedness of an angular logo, the more negatively *strongly committed* consumers rated logo and brand; weakly committed consumers reacted positively. Half of real redesigns in their sample moved rounder. **Müller, Kocher & Crettaz (2013), *JBR* 66(1):82–88, DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2011.07.026:** n = 385 — redesign raises perceived modernity; logo attitude → modernity → brand attitude → loyalty. **Van Grinsven & Das (2014), *Journal of Brand Management*:** brand consciousness lowers openness to substantial change **[title only]**. **Baek et al. (2026), *JRCS* 88:104500:** upside-down logos reduce preference via perceived rebelliousness, especially among conservatives. **Design rule:** evolve, don't replace — keep silhouette and color, change one attribute at a time; expect backlash proportional to degree of change and size of the committed base. Confidence: **moderate-strong**.

## I. Small sizes, favicons, app icons

**31. Logo-specific small-size experiments are scarce; evidence comes from legibility and icon research.** **Legge & Bigelow (2011), *Journal of Vision* 11(5):8:** fluent reading needs x-height ≥ ~0.2° visual angle (critical print size; ≥1.4 mm at 40 cm; fluent range 0.2°–2°). Derived: at ~30 cm phone distance, x-height ≥ ~1.05 mm ≈ 7 CSS px — wordmark text in 16–32 px favicons is unreadable. **Icons:** simple icons are found faster and more accurately than complex ones (McDougall, de Bruijn & Curry 2000; replicated in a 2018 *i-Perception* study, PMC6024531, where the gap shrank with familiarity); concreteness speeds meaning access while complexity slows search (Isherwood, McDougall & Curry 2007, *Human Factors* 49:465–476; "Design standards for icons," *Displays* 2022); flat icons win visual search but skeuomorphic icons are recalled better ("Skeuomorphic or flat?", *Displays* 84, 2024), though a 2020 *Applied Ergonomics* study found skeuomorphic icons and text labels aided search for young and old; distinct hue plus a rounded-square border improved app-icon search (2021, *Int. J. Industrial Ergonomics*). **Lin & Chen (2019), *Electronic Commerce Research* 19:211–229:** unstable-looking app icons lower download intent for prevention-focused users. **Design rule:** build a size system — symbol-only mark with one dominant shape and one owned color for 16–64 px; no text below ~7 px x-height; flat, high-contrast, closed silhouette; distinct hue and rounded-square container for app icons; stable, upright composition; test at 16/32/48/180 px on light and dark. Confidence: **moderate** (strong thresholds, weak logo-specific data).

## J. AI-generated logos and AI as evaluator (2022–2026)

**32. No peer-reviewed experiment on consumer response to AI-generated *logos* was found; adjacent evidence is consistent.**
- **Bellaiche et al. (2023), *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications* 8:42.** Identical AI images labeled human vs AI (n = 149 + 148): human label raised liking (d = 0.17), beauty (0.22), profundity (0.47), worth (0.61); perceived effort drove the human advantage; narrative content helped AI-labeled work.
- **"Art-ificial Intelligence" (arXiv 2303.06217, 2023):** pre-registered — AI disclosure had no effect on stories but hurt emotionally evocative first-person poems; penalties concentrate where content is "distinctly human."
- **Nuremberg Institute for Market Decisions (2024/25):** identical ads labeled "AI-generated" rated less natural/useful; smaller penalty for AI-trusting consumers and high-tech products. ***Administrative Sciences* 15(10):395 (2025):** undisclosed AI and human ad images rated equally; disclosed, human won. Pocol et al. (2024): people cannot reliably tell AI images apart.
- **Xu & Mehta (2022), *JAMS* 50(6):1135–1152:** AI as design source cuts perceived brand essence for emotion-driven luxury (fashion); attenuated when functional value is salient (cars). **To et al. (2025), *Journal of Advertising Research*, "When AI Doesn't Sell Prada":** AI imagery backfires for luxury via authenticity. **Bai, Zhang & Liang (2026), *APJML*, DOI 10.1108/APJML-03-2026-0543:** virtual luxury — AI design raises innovativeness, lowers authenticity, nets to zero; frequent luxury buyers prefer AI, infrequent prefer human. **Xu et al. (2027), *JRCS* 94:104975:** higher WTP for visually *simple* AI designs (restraint → luxuriousness), except for luxury brands. A 2026 survey of 316 Chinese consumers (PMC13086335) found *higher* purchase intent for AI-designed products via novelty and brand effort — a correlational counter-signal.
- **"Struggle Premium" (arXiv 2604.15324, n = 70):** 72.9% would pay more for human-made work; process/effort cues improved AI work too. Magni et al. (2024) and Brüns & Meißner (2024): lower creativity/authenticity when AI is known, reduced if AI is framed as augmenting humans **[secondary citations]**.
- **AI as judge.** "The human-authorship halo" (arXiv 2510.08831): humans +13.7 pp pro-human bias, LLM evaluators +34.3 pp, with label-driven criterion inversion. "Seeing Is Not Perceiving" (arXiv 2609.25677, 2026): GPT-4o-mini/GPT-5.4-mini synthetic consumers reproduced ≤2 of 6 canonical visual-marketing effects (logos, packaging, ads) and once reversed the human pattern; in-context evidence helped means but understated heterogeneity.
**Design rules:** quality parity is attainable; the penalty comes from *disclosure* and *domain* (luxury, emotional, "handmade" positioning; low-AI-trust audiences). Where labeling is required (EU AI Act), frame AI as augmenting a human designer and show process; favor visual restraint; AI provenance is least costly for tech/innovative categories. Never let an LLM alone stand in for consumer testing of a logo. Confidence: **moderate** (strong for disclosure penalty, weak for logo-specific generalization).

**33. Data-driven design.** **Dew, Ansari & Toubia (2022), "Letting Logos Speak," *Marketing Science* 41(2):401–425, DOI 10.1287/mksc.2021.1326:** pixel-level logo features linked to brand descriptions and consumer Aaker ratings via multiview representation learning; supports "brand arithmetic" and predicts reactions to rebrands — a template for validating generated logos against personality targets. **Trehan & Kalro (2024), *JPBM* 33(8):1055–1072** code the top-500 global logos; **Pati et al. (2025), *JBR* 200:115655** review 217 logo papers and confirm the field's shift to meaning-based, deep-learning, and neuromarketing methods.

## K. Evidence-based design rules

1. Start from the high-recognition profile: representational/organic form, balanced but not mirrored, moderately elaborate, one repeated element. [strong]
2. Moderate complexity: too simple is generic (false recognition), too complex is slow on first exposures; scale complexity to expected exposure. [strong]
3. New brands: legible wordmark plus a descriptive category cue; earn the symbol-only mark later. [strong]
4. No descriptive cues in negatively valenced categories. [strong]
5. Round forms for comfort/care/warmth; angular for durability/strength/precision. [strong]
6. Default to symmetry; add asymmetry in proportion to brand excitement. [strong]
7. Stable, upright, grounded composition for trust/safety/efficacy/conservation; tilt and implied motion only for excitement/openness/hedonic brands. [moderate-strong]
8. Frame or badge for risky categories; open marks for low-risk/hedonic ones. [moderate-strong]
9. Flat by default; subtle 3D only for tangible products on clean backgrounds. [moderate]
10. Heavy stroke weight → power/competence/ruggedness; light weight → sophistication/sincerity/femininity. [moderate]
11. Red + saturation → excitement; blue → competence; dark → durability/ruggedness; light → friendliness; black-and-white → status for new premium brands; green/blue → eco/ethical. Verify hue meanings per culture; blue/green/white travel best. [strong for red/blue; moderate otherwise]
12. Type: harmony + naturalness + light flourish raise all dimensions; elaborate trades competence for excitement; sans-serif = potency/utilitarian, serif = activity/hedonic; uppercase = premium/masculine, lowercase = approachable/feminine; handwritten only for emotional, artisanal, new brands. [moderate]
13. Make name sound, shape, and typeface congruent (front vowels ↔ small/light/angular; back vowels ↔ large/heavy/round; round type ↔ sweet/soft). [moderate-strong]
14. Incomplete letterforms only when innovation outranks trust. [strong]
15. Concrete imagery for heritage; abstract for modern positioning. [moderate]
16. Ornament/craft detail signals luxury and innovativeness, but not for familiar brands or basic products. [moderate-strong]
17. Redesign by evolution: keep silhouette and color, change one attribute; expect backlash proportional to change and commitment. [moderate-strong]
18. Memorability = one dominant distinctive silhouette + one owned color; test with draw-from-memory and 1-second recognition. [moderate]
19. Small sizes: symbol-only mark for ≤64 px, no text below ~7 px x-height, high contrast, closed silhouette, distinct hue, rounded-square app container; test at 16/32/48/180 px on light and dark. [moderate]
20. AI provenance: parity in quality, penalty on disclosure, worst for luxury/authentic/emotional positioning; frame as human-augmented, show process, favor restraint; never substitute LLM judgment for consumer tests. [moderate]

## L. Visual feature → Aaker (1997) brand personality

Aaker (1997), *JMR* 34(3):347–356: Sincerity (down-to-earth, honest, wholesome, cheerful), Excitement (daring, spirited, imaginative, up-to-date), Competence (reliable, intelligent, successful), Sophistication (upper-class, charming), Ruggedness (outdoorsy, tough). Cells give the feature setting that raises each dimension; sources in brackets refer to entries above.

| Feature | Sincerity | Excitement | Competence | Sophistication | Ruggedness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Round, organic curves [4, 24] | Angular-dynamic, diagonal, motion [4, 20] | Geometric precision, stable base [4, 9] | Slender refined curves, thin lines [10, 26] | Hard angles, heavy forms [4, 10] |
| Symmetry / stability | Symmetric, upright [6, 9] | Asymmetric (dosed), tilted [5, 6, 9] | Symmetric, stable [6, 7] | Symmetric, harmonious [6] | Grounded; symmetry less critical |
| Complexity | Simple-to-moderate, natural [1] | Moderate-elaborate, active [1, 26] | Simple, flat [18] | Ornamented craft cues (new brands) [18] | Simple, bold [17] |
| Hue | White/pink/light yellow [21, not re-verified]; green for wholesome [23] | Red, orange [21] | Blue; black-and-white for status [21, 23] | Black, purple, monochrome [21, 23] | Brown, dark green [21, 23] |
| Value / saturation | Light, moderate saturation | High saturation [21] | Mid-dark, controlled | Dark or monochrome, low saturation [23] | Dark, low lightness [21, 23] |
| Typeface | Natural/handwritten (new brands), harmonious, lowercase [25, 28] | Elaborate, active, serif, flourish [26, 27] | Sans-serif, uppercase, moderate-heavy, harmonious [26, 27] | Light/extended, serif, flourish [26, 27] | Heavy, condensed sans, uppercase [10, 26] |
| Name sound | Soft fricatives, mid vowels | Front vowels (fast, light) [29] | Stops/voiced consonants (potent) [29] | Front vowels + fricatives (delicate) [29] | Back vowels, stops (large, strong) [29] |
| Frame | Open or gentle badge | Open, unframed [8] | Framed in risky categories [8] | Thin frame or none | Shield/badge [8] |
| Dynamism | Static, calm [20] | Implied motion, drag-force cues, animation [20] | Static or restrained | Slow, restrained | Static, weighty |

Masculinity/femininity (Lieven et al. 2015) overlays this grid: angular + dark + uppercase + geometric = masculine; organic + light/pink + lowercase = feminine [24, 28].

- No logo-specific memorability model; §19 relies on general image-memorability research and one industry study.

- Several 2026–27 JRCS papers (logo facing direction and heritage; upward-tilted logos and unhealthiness; overlapping letters and uniqueness) were seen by title only and are not used as rules.
